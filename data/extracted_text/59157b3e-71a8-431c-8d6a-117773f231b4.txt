Charge and Current: 
Most basic quantity in an electric circuit is the electric charge. 
Charge is an electrical  property of the atomic particles of which matter consists, 
measured in coulombs (C). 
❖ In 1 C of charge, there are 1/(1.602 × 10^−19) = 6.24 × 10^18 electrons.  
❖  1 electronic charge, e = 1.602 × 10^−19 C.  
❖ The law of conservation of charge states that charg e can neither be created nor 
destroyed, only transferred. Thus, the algebraic sum of the electric charges in a 
system does not change. 
 
Electricity - a form of energy that results from the flow of charged particles. 
Electric current - the time rate of change of charge, measured in amperes (A).

an element, measured in volts (V). 
Power - the time rate of expending or absorbing energy, measured in watts (W).

Independent sources 
 
Dependent sources  
2 types of elements found in electric circuits: 
1. Passive elements  
2. Active elements.  
An active element is capable of generating energy while a passive element is not. 
Examples of passive elements are resistors, capacitors, transforms and inductors.  
Examples of active elements include generators, batteries, transistors and operational 
amplifiers. 
● The most important active elements are voltage or current sources that generally 
deliver power to the circuit connected to them.  
There are 2 kinds of sources: 
1. Independent sources 
2. Dependent sources.  
An ideal independent source is an active element that provides a specified voltage 
or current that is completely independent of other circuit elements. 
An ideal dependent (or controlled) source is an active element in which the source 
quantity is controlled by another voltage or current. 
 
Dependent sources are usually designated by diamond-shaped symbols. Since the control 
of the dependent source is achieved by a voltage or current of some other element in the 
circuit, and the source can be voltage or current, there are 4 types of dependent sources, 
namely:  
1. A voltage-controlled voltage source (VCVS).  
2. A current-controlled voltage source (CCVS).  
3. A voltage-controlled current source (VCCS).  
4. A current-controlled current source (CCCS).

Mesh Analysis: 
Mesh analysis provides another general procedure for analyzing circuits, using mesh 
currents as the circuit variables 
Steps to Determine Mesh Currents:  
1. Assign mesh currents i1, i2, . . . , in to the n meshes.  
2. Apply KVL to each of the n meshes. Use Ohm’s law to express the voltages in terms 
of the mesh currents.  
3. Solve the resulting n simultaneous equations to get the mesh currents.
